## Notes on initial Matthew interview

Brighton, couple in their 20s. Had bed bugs before and believe they haven't rid
themselves of them yet. Did not get pest control before. This is their first
appointment with Matthew. He immediately does heat treatment and steaming in the
flat, as he states that he "doesn't do inspections" and "if you got bed bugs, you
know you got". But he later retracts that statement as we discuss delusional clients.

We can't find any bed bugs in the hollow metal of the bed frame.

You can find them on the bed or sofa usually. Matthew says the sofa is usually with
old people.

They are harder to find when there are few of them.

We talk about the lorry drivers, who bed bugs are common with, and public
transportation, through which bed bugs tend to spread. I mention the Eurostar
incident. Matthew says he reckons that's where the resistance came from.

Most of the bugs get smart to it. Guess it's just evolution. Even the mice start
avoiding the boxes ('box shy').

Matthew had been in pest control since he left school. His father too worked in the
same field. Matthew works with falcons (for pigeons) and dogs (for mice and rats, not
bed bugs, as some exterminators use dogs for bed bug detection through pheromones)
and employs a falconer. He says field biologists ('pesties' he calls them) might be
more keen to let me in for research. Apparently cavemen got bed bugs from bats
hundreds of millions of years ago.

The bedroom's window is double glazed, making it ideal for effectively turning the
room into an oven. The goal temperature is 70–80 degrees C for at least four hours;
in a lab environment an hour is sufficient. Matthew sets a reflective sheet for
insulation and blasts steam from a gas balloon from the bottom. He lets me check the
room's temperature with an infrared thermostat as it's rapidly rising. It's fun.

The kitchen and living room area is less ideal for heat treatment as house
construction in the UK is shit. This is a Victorian house. So he goes over with an
industrial steamer over the couch (which turns out to also open as a bed) and a few
boxes. He mentions that buying one can be a more cost-effective alternative to
getting an exterminator as it is at the price point of only a few hundred quid.

Today's treatment, for both steaming and heat I imagine, the cost of treatment is
1200 GBP.

Apparently you can send specimens of bed bug tails (because they have them) to
Reading university.

As he steams different items he sets them aside on the dining table. There are a lot
of scarves, jackets scattered in the room that could carry bed bugs. I can't help but
think of cross contamination. Matthew however is unbothered. Apparently, bed bug
infestations are a common issue with lorry drivers. Matthew lets his son play in the
lorry at the end of a treatment. He asks me if I want to have a go with the steamer.
I am thrilled. When it's done Matthew sits on the couch. He says that he only brought
bed bugs home with him once, because he was careless. In general he doesn't do the
suit. I asked if he takes any safety measures when he gets home or to his car; he
doesn't ("do you sit on your couch straight away?"). He is very resilient in that
sense, as I would have expected that sense of paranoia to rub off on you doing that
line of work. (Note: I can think of zoologists, science writers who had developed an
obsession with an infestation and even Delusions of Parasitosis. Abundance of
knowledge does seem to be detrimental in that context.)

Council care homes and children's homes are vulnerable to infestations. Councils have
tried to save on pest control by prioritising getting contractors to exterminate one
type of pest over the other locally. But you can't save on pest control. Matthew
mentioned a case where the housing association knowingly moved a family to a bed bug
infested home against his advice. The family, which had four children, had to stay
for lack of choice.

The paranoia of infestations doesn't rub off on Matthew. He says he only had them
once. He doesn't wear a hazmat suit nor change his clothes when coming home. Just
sits on his couch. Infestations are common with lorry drivers; Matthew lets his son
play in the lorry after he's done with treatment. You have to be meticulous steaming
the seams front and back to ensure they are all gone.

As the heat treatment is going, he waits and scrolls, mainly to make sure the house
doesn't burn down. He says that it's a different type of heat: when you take a breath
you feel it drying your lungs. In Russia they used to sleep with the door open to
kill bed bugs by freezing. But were the people in the room as well?

Something about soaking cloth in paraffin as bed bug treatment?

He tells me of an old lady who was feeding the foxes, and another old lady who was
council ordered to clear up rat droppings from her house floor. When Matthew got
there she had already removed several trash bags worth of dry droppings on her own.
The build up was 10–15 years of droppings. The council charged her with the money
they might need for contractors for treatment of the house but not for the help she
needed. For all Matthew knows, she is still in that house.

Worst case of bed bugs he saw was in a student house. Bed bugs were harbouring on the
mattress and walls.

Wednesday's appointment in west London would be at an older lady's house. It would be
a steaming treatment. I am curious to see how her interaction with Matthew would go,
as she fits the DP patient profile as well as belongs to a demographic that's more
vulnerable to infestations.

There's a communal garden and a separate grassy area where two gardeners are
currently working. One of the kindergarten teachers is there briefly. I have been

She calls her manager, but her card doesn't work on that door. One of the gardeners
pitches in; his card does allow entrance to the building. I am embarrassed for not
asking him beforehand. My research has to do with blue collar workers (like pest
control, zoology and entomology; gardening and landscape architecture is a nice
intersection).

The professor is nice. I tell him of my research. He refers me to a documentary that
comments on another film regarding a fictitious, or delusional, disease. It explores
it through internet comments as input. It might be interesting to use all of these.

He gives me simple and incredibly effective technical documentation methods. Lock
brightness on phone when filming. Hold and draw out when filming, as you would likely
scrap the beginning and end of the clips you might use. Gather visuals of the
environment for the b-roll to avoid jumps in my video. The environment complements
what transpires verbally in the interview. Does it contrast what is said? Provide
further insight into the person?

I ask how I might get consent and a potential interview with an older lady who had
booked a steaming treatment with the pest control person I will shadow tomorrow in
west London. He says that I should be clear on not meaning to shame or judge.

Nonverbal encouragement such as fervent nodding, to avoid damaging audio recordings.
Use silence to encourage further details in the interviewee's answers. Start with
chatting to build confidence and build up to specific questions, and to encourage
more abstract thinking. Avoiding technically disruptive noises but also incorporating
them in the outcome.

He suggests I record phone calls I'd make to pest control companies as interview
requests.

A TA drops by and Professor Leizeola tells her briefly of my project. "I can already
feel them crawling on me," she says. Later on, as I watch *Watching the Pain of
Others*, I think that it's such a simple way to express empathy.

I've already established that there are equivalences between biology and computation:
emergence and complexity, fractals, particle systems, neural networks. But what is
the intersection between computation and empathy?

## On Morgellons and DP

I'd like to log a thought I am afraid to lose. I came across *Watching the Pain of
Others*, a video essay of a researcher's reaction to Penny Lane's documentary,
through which I learned about Morgellons disease, a condition that was coined around
2003 outside the medical field and, similar to Delusional Parasitosis, also involves
a belief of a parasitic infestation. The difference is Morgellons is self-diagnosed
and thrives on the internet as 'patients' (no disrespect, just a distinction)
document their symptoms and treatment methods, while DP is an official diagnosis.

(Note: the anthropology professor suggested that, similar to the researcher and
documentarian, I use online accounts for insights on DP patients. The issue could be
that since it's a diagnosis outside of the patient's choice, they would not announce
themselves as DP patients. There are researched accounts linked to DP patients trying
to prove their sanity, such as a zoologist who published a book of 20 years of
research into the parasite that plagued her.)

There is little distinction otherwise between Morgellons and DP, with these two
coined terms being contradictory to each other and having intersections. Both occur
predominantly in women. Self-diagnosis and DIY methods seem to be a way for these
women to regain agency over this aspect of their life.

The researcher noted that Morgellons is a condition where you observe your body and
ask why, and a disease born of excessive empathy. By the end of the essay she
contacts Penny Lane for an interview, not quite knowing why, but summing it up as
'needing to be seen'. She contemplates reaching out to the three subjects of the
film, who are self-diagnosed Morgellons patients, and decides against it due to the
detrimental effects on her of the parasocial relationship born out of her research
into their accounts.

It made me think of the parasocial angle in computing and empathy, as well as my own
want to interview individuals who deal with infestation (a group which might include
the DP sector as well). Maybe I should contact the researcher as well, to be seen by
someone who understands, rather than being stuck in a feedback loop of paranoia. The
frustrating thing is DP had turned out in some cases to be a misdiagnosis.
After-effects of infestations are completely logical in their paranoia-inducing
facets.

Another important note: I think it's important for me to contact the women who
research this subject. It needs to be very intentional. I think there's a reason this
subject touches us, and there is an 'us' in this, as women maybe, but I think it goes
beyond that, as a form of introspection. So you need to be very intentional with the
connections you make, who you are looking into and would look back at you.

## After death clean

I get to the address before Matthew. He tells me on the phone to enter through the
side alley and the front door.

I am joining an after death clean up, where a person's body has been found only after
a while. Liquid and debris seep into furniture and further down.

The neighbour is the first to greet me. He is dumbfounded when I ask about the pest
control people. I guess it would be an insult to be disposed of in the same manner as
pests and trash.

Warren, the person on that day, says it is his first time on this kind of job. He is
visibly shaken by it. The nature of the job is sawing into, dismantling any piece of
the house the body had amalgamated with. Then placing them in bags.

As I am transcribing later, much later, Warren says on the recording of the
deceased's body lying there that his 'body fluid just gone through to heaven'. I am
not sure why I think it's beautiful.

I ask how long has it been? Warren doesn't know. He doesn't know how long it takes a
body to do that. He was told it's been roughly a week. Matthew told me it's been a
couple of months. It's March. Calendar has logs up to mid January.

I think of the uncanny. There is something charming about the incongruity of the
collections I find across the house: a blender next to medical records, a couch
manual, art equipment. But at a certain point the state of clutter becomes too much.
I wonder how it might have felt for his house to turn on him; the familiar books
hindering movement in the house; the medical equipment becoming an insult; the now
vacant bedrooms upstairs functioning as storage units.

Matthew told me the week before to expect an insect buildup.

'Somewhere being a place you are left in.'

Matthew says most of these cases have carers, though they 'obviously don't do much'.

Happens often. Claims the oldest cases are two years, though he did cite it as
'nearly 12 months' in the interview last week, when he extended the invitation.

24 was the youngest case.

## 11.8 — not the lab visit I had in mind

The education department has a PGCE practice lab. The technician, Dave, was kind
enough to agree to lend me some lab equipment ('glassware', as I call it) for the
degree show. There are many 'odd and wonderful things' here, as Dave says. And he
takes me on a tour.

I called him David in the email, which only his mother called him. He said there's
only so much to do after retiring, and Goldsmiths can't sack him because the lab
wouldn't exist without him. He used to work in something something bioengineering
something something crystallisation, proteins, HIV cure. Very cool. I knew I wouldn't
recount this information properly. 'Can I record this conversation?' (Man, the
researcher that I've become in my very limited experience.) He gets flustered, I drop
it. Clearly only made so much progress.

We talk about the bed bugs. Apparently Dave does steam cleaning himself.

There are, I'm pleased to find, some insects in jars that would fit perfectly for my
thing. There's a lovely old computer Dave is offering. I want it, but would be
homeless with the contents of my flat to carry by the time the exhibition rolls
around.

I collect stuff in a rack: beakers and jars and test tubes. I'm being offered rusty
forceps as a gift. For the life of me I'm unsure what I might do with them beyond the
exhibition, but nonetheless I am thrilled.

I am getting anxious when the first hour rolls around and I realise that I don't know
what I want exactly from this abundance of resources. I should be coding at the
library now. Or writing. Or working. But at least I have a nice collection of things,
odd and wonderful.
